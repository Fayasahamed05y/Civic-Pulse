Every pothole starts as somebody's complaint.

Most civic issues die in a phone queue: no reference number, no tracking, no way for a municipal team to tell a burst pipe from a chipped kerb. Civic Pulse gives residents a five-minute report form and a running score, and gives the people fixing things a triaged, de-duplicated work order board.

The gap between reporting and fixing

Two separate failures compound each other, and a single form can't fix either on its own.

Residents have no reliable channel

Calls go to busy lines, complaints get logged on paper registers or scattered social posts, and there's no ticket number to follow up with — so people stop reporting altogether.

Municipal teams can't prioritise

Even where reports do arrive, staff see them in the order they land, not in order of risk. A cosmetic garbage complaint can sit ahead of a live wire near a school simply because it came in first.

How the urgency score works

A lightweight, explainable heuristic — the kind of thing that would be swapped for a trained classifier once real report data exists.

Category weight

Each category starts with a base risk score — a water leak or exposed wire starts higher than a garbage complaint, reflecting typical safety impact.

Risk language

The description is scanned for terms like "accident", "school", "children" or "exposed wire" — each one raises the score, the way a triage nurse weighs a patient's own words.

Duplicate clustering

New reports are compared against open issues in the same category. A close match adds a corroborating vote to the existing ticket instead of creating a new one.
